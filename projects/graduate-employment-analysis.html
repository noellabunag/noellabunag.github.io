Graduate Employment Outcomes & Work-Life Balance Analysis

Analysed Singapore’s graduate employment outcomes, salary trends, and working hours using Python, data visualisation, and automated reporting workflows.

01

Research Question

How do graduate employment outcomes and salaries relate to work-life balance in Singapore, as reflected by usual working hours?

Graduate salaries get a lot of attention, but pay is only one part of working life. This project looked at how graduate employment outcomes and salaries have changed over time, and how those changes sit alongside trends in working hours. A second goal was to make the reporting repeatable through automation.

02

Data Sources

The analysis combined three sources:

  • Graduate Employment Survey data, supplied as two files (degree details, and employment and salary figures) that were joined on a shared key: 5,135 records.
  • Ministry of Manpower labour force statistics on usual hours worked, by year.
  • Ministry of Manpower labour force statistics on employed residents by education level, used to track the number of degree holders.

03

Data Cleaning

The data needed several steps before it could be analysed with Python and Pandas:

  • Merged the two graduate survey files with an inner join on their shared key.
  • Converted year, employment-rate and salary columns from text to numbers, turning invalid entries into missing values.
  • Removed 479 records with no salary data, leaving 4,656 records covering 6 universities, 69 schools and 302 degree programmes.
  • Extracted the working-hours and degree-holder figures from the Ministry of Manpower spreadsheets, which needed header rows skipped and columns renamed.

Feature engineering

  • Salary categories (below $3,500, $3,500–$5,000, above $5,000).
  • Salary range: the gap between the 75th and 25th percentile salary.
  • Employment bands based on overall and full-time permanent employment rates.

04

Exploratory Analysis

The exploratory analysis looked at how graduate salaries are distributed, how employment rates relate to salaries, and how salaries, working hours and the number of degree holders moved over time.

One finding was that employment rate and salary were only weakly related (correlation of about 0.31): high employment rates appeared across all salary levels.

Key Findings

+27.3%graduate median salary, from about $3,300 (2013) to $4,200 (2022)
−7.08%average weekly working hours, from 48 to 44.6
r = −0.93correlation between the yearly salary and working-hour series

Over the same period, the number of degree holders in the resident labour force grew from about 649,000 (2013) to about 1,000,000 (2023), an increase of roughly 54%.

These are findings from an academic analysis of yearly data for 2013–2022. The strong negative correlation describes how the two trends moved together over that period. It does not show that one caused the other.

07

UiPath Automation Workflow

The notebook exports its cleaned datasets as CSV files for the automation to use. To make the reporting repeatable, I built a UiPath workflow that runs the analysis scripts and sends the results out by email, so the report can be produced without doing each step by hand.

09

Lessons Learned

  • Cleaning shapes the conclusions. Getting separate datasets to line up properly was necessary before any comparison between them meant anything.
  • Correlation needs careful wording. A strong relationship between two trends is interesting, but it has to be reported as a pattern rather than as a cause.
  • Automation pays off quickly. Once the analysis ran from a workflow, re-running the report became a single step.
